Living in Spain

Expats in Spain: What Is Life Like After the Move?

By Aleksandra Kobus · 9 May 2025 · updated 26 October 2026 · 4 min read

In short: Most expats settle into Spain within the first year, but the first months take work. Expect paperwork (padrón, residence registration, health card, tax residence after 183 days), slower bureaucracy with appointments (cita previa), a later daily rhythm, and a job market with unemployment around 10%, where remote work and your own business are common. Costs on the coast are lower than in most of northern Europe, except for housing in hotspots. Learning Spanish and joining local clubs make the biggest difference.

Every year hundreds of thousands of people from other European countries and beyond make Spain their home. The reasons are similar: the climate, the outdoor life, the food, and often a better balance between work and free time. But the move is only the beginning. What is daily life like once the boxes are unpacked?

This guide covers what our clients tell us about their first year, from the paperwork to making friends. For the steps before the move, see our moving to Spain checklist.

The first weeks: paperwork

  • Padrón – register at the town hall with your address. You need it for almost everything.
  • Residence – EU citizens staying longer than three months register at the foreign nationals' office or police and receive a green registration certificate. Non-EU citizens need a visa or permit and then a TIE card.
  • Healthcare – once you work, pay into the system or bring a pension form (S1), you can get a health card from your local health centre.
  • Bank and utilities – a Spanish bank account makes paying bills, community fees and local taxes much easier.
  • Driving – EU licences stay valid; many non-EU licences must be exchanged within six months of becoming resident.
  • Taxes – after more than 183 days a year in Spain you are normally tax resident and declare your worldwide income here.

Most offices work by appointment (cita previa), often booked online. Patience and copies of every document help a lot. Read more about staying longer than 90 days and healthcare in Spain.

Work and the job market

Spain's unemployment rate is around 10%, higher than the EU average, and salaries are lower than in northern Europe. That is why many expats bring their work with them: remote employees, freelancers (autónomos) and business owners. Tourism, property, healthcare, IT and international schools also hire people with languages. Some newcomers who move for a job can use the special tax regime for inbound workers. See the best-paid jobs in Spain.

The cost of living

Day-to-day costs on the Costa Blanca and Costa del Sol are lower than in most of Germany, the Netherlands, Scandinavia or the UK: fresh food at the market, eating out at lunchtime and public transport are good value. Housing is the exception: rents in Málaga, Marbella, Alicante and popular coastal towns have risen fast. Electricity can be expensive in summer if you use air conditioning a lot. See our cost of living guide.

Language and the daily rhythm

In the big resorts you can manage in English, but in offices, health centres and smaller towns Spanish matters, and in the Valencian Community you will also see Valencian. Even basic Spanish makes daily life easier and opens doors to friendships. The day runs later than in northern Europe: lunch is around 2 pm, dinner after 9 pm, and in smaller towns many shops close in the early afternoon. Summers are slower, and August is a holiday month.

Making friends and finding your community

Spaniards are open and sociable, and life happens outdoors: on terraces, in squares and at local festivals. The quickest way in is through shared activities: sports clubs, walking and cycling groups, golf, choirs, language exchanges, volunteering and your children's school. Most coastal towns also have international associations where newcomers help each other.

The most common challenges

  • Bureaucracy – appointments, different rules in different regions and documents in Spanish. A good gestor or lawyer saves time.
  • Language – the first months can feel tiring; it gets better quickly with lessons and practice.
  • Quiet winters – some resorts are very calm out of season. Choose a town that lives all year.
  • Homesickness – normal, especially in the first winter. Cheap flights help, and so does building a routine.
  • Housing – renting first, for six to twelve months, is the best way to be sure about the area before you buy.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to settle in Spain?

Most people say the first six months are the hardest because of paperwork and language. By the end of the first year most expats feel at home.

Do I need to speak Spanish to live in Spain?

Not in the big international resorts, but for offices, healthcare and making local friends it helps a lot. Basic Spanish is worth learning before or soon after the move.

When do I become tax resident in Spain?

Normally when you spend more than 183 days a year in Spain, or when your main business or family interests are here. Then you declare your worldwide income in Spain.

Where do most expats live in Spain?

Madrid and Barcelona for work, and on the coast mostly the Costa Blanca and Costa del Sol, where there are large international communities.
