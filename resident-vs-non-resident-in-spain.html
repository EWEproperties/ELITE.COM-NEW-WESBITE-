Tax Resident or Non-Resident in Spain – What Changes for Property Owners

By Anita Orłowska · 10 February 2026 · updated 24 October 2026 · 5 min read

In short: You are a Spanish tax resident if you spend more than 183 days a year in Spain, if your main business or economic interests are in Spain, or (as a presumption) if your spouse and minor children live there. Buying a home alone does not make you resident. Residents pay tax on their worldwide income at progressive rates, roughly 19% to 47–54% depending on region. Non-residents pay only on Spanish income: 19% (EU/EEA) or 24% (others) on rent, an imputed tax on homes they do not rent out, and 19% on gains when they sell.

Most buyers think about the sun and the sea first and about tax much later. Yet whether Spain sees you as a resident or a non-resident decides which income Spain taxes, at what rates, and whether Spain is interested in what you earn at home.

This guide explains when you become a tax resident, how each group is taxed, and the mistakes that most often cause problems.

When are you a tax resident in Spain?

Tax residency does not depend on your nationality or on owning property. It depends on facts. Under Spanish law you are a tax resident for a calendar year if:

  • you spend more than 183 days in Spain in that year (short trips abroad still count as days in Spain unless you prove tax residence in another country), or
  • the main centre of your business or economic interests is in Spain, or
  • your spouse (not legally separated) and minor children live in Spain. This is a presumption you can rebut.

If you also count as resident in your home country, the double tax treaty between the two countries decides, looking in turn at where you have a permanent home, where your personal and economic life is centred, where you usually live and your nationality.

Tax residency is not the same as legal residence. EU citizens who stay more than three months must register as residents, and non-EU citizens need a visa. Our guide to staying in Spain for more than 90 days covers that side.

How residents are taxed

Spanish tax residents pay income tax (IRPF) on their worldwide income: salary, pensions, business income, rent, interest, dividends and capital gains, wherever they come from. They file a yearly return between April and June. Tax already paid abroad is credited under the double tax treaty.

  • Work, pension and rental income is taxed on a progressive scale shared by the State and the region. It starts at about 19% and the top rate is about 47% in Andalusia and close to 54% in the Valencian Community.
  • Savings income (interest, dividends and capital gains) is taxed at 19% up to €6,000, then 21%, 23%, 27% and 30% above €300,000.
  • Residents can deduct rental costs and get allowances, and gains on selling a main home are tax-free if reinvested in a new main home, or if the seller is over 65.
  • Residents with more than €50,000 in assets abroad in one category (accounts, investments or property) must report them on form 720.
  • Wealth tax applies to worldwide assets, with allowances set by each region. In the Valencian Community the allowance rises from €1 million to €2 million from tax year 2026.

People who move to Spain for work may qualify for the special regime for impatriates (the "Beckham law"), with a flat 24% on Spanish work income up to €600,000 for six years.

How non-residents are taxed

Non-residents pay non-resident income tax (IRNR) only on income from Spain, using form 210. For a property owner that means:

  • Rental income: 19% for residents of the EU, Norway and Iceland, who can deduct costs such as IBI, community fees, repairs and interest. Everyone else, including UK, Swiss and US owners, pays 24% on gross rent with no deductions.
  • A home you do not rent out: tax on an imputed income of 1.1% of the cadastral value (2% if not revised recently), at the same 19% or 24%. This is due every year, by 31 December of the following year.
  • Selling: 19% on the gain. The buyer holds back 3% of the price and pays it to the tax office on your account. If that is more than your tax, you claim the difference back.

Non-residents get no personal allowances and do not file the normal yearly return. They also pay wealth tax on their Spanish assets above the allowance. Our guide to property and wealth taxes in Spain has the details.

Neither status is better in itself. For one holiday home and income earned at home, non-resident status is usually simpler and cheaper. If you live and work in Spain, you are a resident whether you like it or not.

Common mistakes

Most problems come from wrong assumptions rather than from the rates:

  • Thinking a home you do not rent costs no tax. Non-residents pay the imputed tax every year, even if the home stands empty.
  • Not counting days. Long winters plus summer holidays can pass 183 days. Keep travel records.
  • Working remotely from Spain for months while declaring residence elsewhere.
  • Moving your life to Spain (family, car, doctor, social life) while your tax papers still say you live at home.
  • Missing the 3% when selling and not claiming back the excess.

The result can be back taxes, interest and fines, and sometimes being taxed twice until the two countries sort it out.

Frequently asked questions

Does buying a house in Spain make me a tax resident?

No. Owning property does not make you resident. What counts is where you spend more than 183 days a year, where your main economic interests are and, as a presumption, where your spouse and minor children live.

How much tax does a non-resident pay on a Spanish holiday home?

If you do not rent it out, you pay 19% (EU/EEA) or 24% (others) on an imputed income of 1.1–2% of the cadastral value, plus the local IBI tax. For a typical apartment this is often around €150–400 a year.

Can I be tax resident in two countries at once?

Both countries can claim you under their own rules. The double tax treaty then decides where you are resident, based on your permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual residence and nationality.

Do non-residents have to file a tax return every year?

Yes, if they own property in Spain. Form 210 covers both the imputed income on a home you do not rent and any rental income.

Which status is better?

It depends on where you really live and work. A non-resident with one holiday home usually pays less. If you live in Spain, residency is not a choice but a consequence.
