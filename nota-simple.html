Nota Simple in Spain – What It Shows and How to Read It

By Anita Orłowska · 20 January 2026 · updated 24 October 2026 · 4 min read

In short: The Nota Simple is a short extract from the Spanish Land Registry. It shows who owns a property, its registered description and any mortgages or other charges. Anyone can order one online for about €9, usually within a day. It is the first check before you pay a deposit, but it does not show unpaid IBI or community fees, illegal extensions or who is living in the property, so your lawyer checks those separately.

Before you sign anything in Spain, you or your lawyer will look at the Nota Simple. It looks like a dry official extract, but a few lines in it tell you whether a purchase is straightforward or needs more work. Here is how to read it.

What is a Nota Simple?

The Nota Simple Informativa is an information extract from the Land Registry (Registro de la Propiedad). It shows the current legal state of a property: who owns it, how it is described and what charges are registered against it.

It is used at every step of a purchase: before the reservation and deposit contract (contrato de arras), by the bank when it approves a mortgage, and by the notary before the deed of sale is signed. The registry is public, so anyone with a legitimate interest, such as a buyer, can request one.

How to get one

  • Online on the Registradores de España website. You can search by address, registry number (finca registral) or cadastral reference, and pay about €9 by card. Most arrive by email within a working day.
  • In person at the Land Registry office for the area.
  • Through your lawyer or agent, which is how most buyers get it.

Use a recent one. Banks and notaries want a Nota Simple no more than a few weeks old, and the notary checks the registry again electronically just before signing.

How to read it

Four parts matter most:

  • Descripción de la finca. The property's registered description: address, type, floor area, plot size and the share in common areas. Compare it with what you are actually buying.
  • Titularidad. Who owns it, in what shares and how they acquired it (purchase, inheritance, gift). The person selling must be the registered owner, or have authority from all owners.
  • Cargas. The charges: mortgages, seizures (embargos), easements, rights of others and court notes. Ideally it says libre de cargas, free of charges.
  • Documentos pendientes. Documents submitted to the registry but not yet recorded. These can change the picture, so ask what they are.

Entries that need care

  • A mortgage. Common and not a problem in itself. The seller must pay it off at or before the signing, and the cancellation must be registered. Your lawyer arranges this with the seller's bank at the notary.
  • Seizures (embargos). A creditor has a claim on the property. It must be lifted before you buy.
  • Several owners or an inheritance. Every owner must agree to sell, and any inheritance must be fully settled and registered first.
  • Pending documents. Something is about to change, for example a new owner or a new charge.
  • Size differences. If the registered area is much smaller than the real house, something may have been built without being registered or licensed.

What looks worrying but often is not

Old mortgages often stay on the registry after they have been repaid, simply because nobody registered the cancellation. Your lawyer asks the bank for a certificate and has it removed. Small differences between the registry and the cadastre are also common and often come from old measuring methods rather than illegal works.

What the Nota Simple does not show

  • Whether IBI and community fees are paid. Ask for the latest IBI receipt and a certificate from the community administrator; unpaid amounts can pass to the new owner.
  • Whether every part of the house, such as a pool, terrace or extension, has a building licence. Compare the property with the cadastre and check with the town hall.
  • Whether someone is living in it, such as a tenant or squatter. See it in person.
  • The physical condition of the property. Consider a survey for an older home.

For a high-value purchase, your lawyer may also order the Certificado Registral, the full certificate with the property's registry history, which has full legal value.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a Nota Simple cost?

About €9 online through the Registradores de España website. Most arrive by email within one working day.

Can I get a Nota Simple for any property?

Yes, if you have a legitimate interest, such as planning to buy it. The registry is public.

What does libre de cargas mean?

Free of charges: no mortgage, seizure or other charge is registered against the property.

What is the difference between a Nota Simple and a Certificado Registral?

The Nota Simple is an information extract showing the current state. The Certificado Registral is a certified document with the full history, used in court or for high-value purchases.

The first check, not the last

A clean Nota Simple is a good start, but a safe purchase also checks taxes, community fees, licences and the property itself. New builds bought from a developer are usually simpler, as there is no history of owners or old charges.
